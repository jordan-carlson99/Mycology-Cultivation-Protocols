## Safety Information

[Follow general safety practices](/general-information/saftey/general-safety.md)

### Known Risks

- Respiratory exposure to airborne spores during fruiting
- Bacterial contamination risk (wet rot/"wet rot" contamination) if grain is over-hydrated
- Mold contamination (green, black, or pink) if sterilization is insufficient
- Cross-contamination between cultures during inoculation
- Burns from pressure cooker steam

### Containment Requirements

- Still Air Box (SAB) or Laminar Flow Hood required for all sterile procedures
- Pressure cooker capable of reaching 15 PSI
- Clean workspace separated from high-traffic areas
- All transfers performed in sterile environment

### Safety Equipment

- Nitrile or latex gloves
- N95 or equivalent respirator during grain handling and fruiting
- Safety goggles when handling boiling water or pressure cooker
- 70% isopropyl alcohol for surface sterilization
- Heat-resistant gloves for pressure cooker handling

### Emergency Procedures

- If contamination is observed: immediately remove and seal affected jars in plastic bag before disposal
- If burned by steam: run cool water over affected area for 10+ minutes
- If grain is wet-rotted (sour smell, black/slimy grain): dispose of in sealed bag outdoors

### Storage Safety

- Finished grain spawn can be stored in refrigerator (2-4°C / 35-40°F) for up to 4 weeks
- Keep lids tight during storage to prevent desiccation
- Label all jars with species, strain, and date of inoculation

### Disposal Requirements

- Contaminated grain must be sealed in a plastic bag before disposal
- Do not compost contaminated grain — heat-treat (boil or autoclave) if composting is desired
- Spent (fully fruited) spawn blocks can be composted or used in outdoor beds

## Required Materials

### Essential Materials

- Wild Bird Seed (millet-based, no sunflower seeds or pelleted food — check for milo, millet, canary seed)
- Wide-mouth quart mason jars (or pint jars for smaller batches)
- Pressure cooker (capable of 15 PSI)
- Jar lids with self-healing injection ports and polyfill filter discs, OR standard lids modified with a nail
- 70% isopropyl alcohol
- Still Air Box (SAB)
- Alcohol lamp, torch, or lighter
- Spore syringe or liquid culture
- Micropore tape or polyfill (for filter patches)
- Tin foil (for covering lids during sterilization)

### Optional Materials

- Gypsum (1 tablespoon per quart jar — helps prevent grain clumping)
- Magnetic stir bar and stir plate (for liquid culture preparation)
- Flow hood (instead of SAB for improved sterile conditions)
- Injection port lids (self-healing silicone ports for easier inoculation)
- Polyfill or Tyvek filter discs for gas exchange

### Recommended Suppliers

- Wild Bird Seed: Local farm supply, Walmart, or Tractor Supply (as of 2026-MAR: ~$0.50-1.00/lb)
- Mason jars: Walmart, Target, or hardware stores (as of 2026-MAR: ~$1.00-2.00/jar)
- Micropore tape: Pharmacy (as of 2026-MAR: ~$3.00/roll)

## Protocol

### Step 1: Grain Selection and Preparation

**Trigger/Timing:** Initial setup — performed the day before sterilization

#### Procedure:

1. Select appropriate wild bird seed:
   - Choose millet-dominated mix (milo, millet, canary grass seed are ideal)
   - Avoid seeds with sunflower seeds (too oily) or pelleted/coated seeds
   - Inspect for mold or insect damage before use

2. Rinse grain thoroughly:
   - Pour grain into large pot or bucket
   - Rinse 2-3 times with cold water, draining each time
   - Stir vigorously and pour off floating debris (empty hulls float — discard them)

3. Boil/simmer grain:
   - Add rinsed grain to pot with enough water to cover by 3-4 inches
   - Bring to a boil, then reduce to simmer
   - Simmer for 15-20 minutes until grain is plump but NOT split or mushy
   - Individual seeds should absorb water but retain their shape

4. Drain and dry:
   - Drain grain through colander
   - Spread evenly on clean towels or baking sheets
   - Allow to surface-dry for 30-60 minutes — grain should look matte, not wet/shiny
   - Air or fan-dry until surface moisture is gone; grain should not stick together when squeezed

**Quality Indicators:**

- Grain is plump and pliable but not split or overcooked
- Surface appears dry/matte, not wet or glistening
- No visible mold or debris
- Grain separates freely when stirred (not clumping)

**Control Points:**

- Do not over-boil grain — split kernels lead to starchy slurry that promotes bacterial contamination
- Ensure grain is surface-dry before loading jars (wet grain = "wet rot" contamination)
- Remove all floating debris (empty hulls, dust) before loading

**Troubleshooting:**

| Problem | Cause | Solution |
| ------- | ----- | -------- |
| Split or mushy grain after boiling | Overcooked | Reduce simmer time; remove from heat sooner |
| Grain still clumping when dried | Insufficient drying time | Spread thinner layer; fan-dry longer |
| Foul odor from grain | Old/spoiled grain | Start fresh with new bag |

### Step 2: Jar Filling and Lid Preparation

**Trigger/Timing:** After grain has surface-dried

#### Procedure:

1. Prepare jar lids:
   - For simple modified lids: use hammer and nail to punch 1 hole per lid
   - For filter patch lids: drill or punch hole, cover with polyfill or Tyvek patch secured with hot glue or silicone
   - For injection port lids: install self-healing silicone port and a filter patch

2. Optionally add gypsum:
   - Mix 1 tablespoon of gypsum per quart of dry grain before filling jars
   - Gypsum prevents grain from fusing together during sterilization

3. Fill jars:
   - Fill jars to 50-66% capacity (no more than 2/3 full)
   - Overfilling reduces gas exchange and makes colonization harder to observe
   - Tap jar gently to settle grain
   - Do not compact or pack grain

4. Cover lids with tin foil:
   - Wrap foil over lids to protect filter patches during sterilization
   - Ensure foil is secured but not so tight it prevents steam penetration around lid edges

**Quality Indicators:**

- Jars filled 50-66% with dry-looking, separated grain
- Lids secured with functioning gas exchange (filter patch or polyfill) and, if used, injection port
- Foil wrapped securely around lid area

**Control Points:**

- Do not overfill jars — gas exchange is critical for healthy colonization
- Ensure all components are clean and free of contamination before filling
- Check that filter patches are intact and properly secured

### Step 3: Sterilization

**Trigger/Timing:** After jars are filled and capped

#### Procedure:

1. Load pressure cooker:
   - Place a trivet, rack, or jar rings on the bottom of the pressure cooker
   - Add water as specified by your pressure cooker model (typically 1.5-2 quarts)
   - Arrange jars on the rack — they should not touch each other or the sides if possible
   - Jars should be above the water line

2. Bring to pressure:
   - Secure pressure cooker lid
   - Heat on high until steam begins to vent
   - Allow to vent for 10 minutes to purge air
   - Apply weight/seal and bring to 15 PSI

3. Sterilize:
   - Maintain 15 PSI for **90-120 minutes** (1.5-2 hours)
   - Grain spawn requires longer sterilization than BRF cakes due to higher nutrient content
   - Monitor heat source to maintain steady pressure

4. Cool and rest:
   - Remove from heat after sterilization time
   - Allow pressure to drop naturally — do not force-cool
   - Let jars rest undisturbed for minimum 8-12 hours (overnight preferred)
   - Jars should be at room temperature before inoculation

**Growth Parameters:**

- Temperature: 121°C (250°F) at 15 PSI
- Time: 90-120 minutes at 15 PSI

**Quality Indicators:**

- Jars reach and maintain 15 PSI for full sterilization window
- No signs of contamination after cooling
- Grain retains structure — not mushy or blackened

**Control Points:**

- Do not open pressure cooker while under pressure
- Ensure full cooling before inoculation — injecting into hot grain damages spores/mycelium
- Allow extended rest time (12-24 hours) before inoculation to maximize sterility assessment

**Troubleshooting:**

| Problem | Cause | Solution |
| ------- | ----- | -------- |
| Wet rot contamination (sour smell, slimy black grain) | Grain over-hydrated or bacterial endospores survived | Ensure grain is surface-dry; extend sterilization time to 2.5 hours |
| Jars cracking in PC | Thermal shock or jar defects | Use room-temperature jars; avoid rapid temperature changes |
| Pressure won't hold at 15 PSI | Faulty gasket or damaged cooker | Inspect and replace gasket; service pressure cooker |

### Step 4: Inoculation

**Trigger/Timing:** After jars have cooled to room temperature (8-24 hours after sterilization)

#### Procedure:

1. Prepare sterile workspace:
   - Wipe down SAB interior with 70% isopropyl alcohol
   - Allow alcohol to evaporate before use (30-60 seconds)
   - Wear gloves wiped with isopropyl alcohol
   - Minimize movements and drafts around SAB

2. Prepare inoculant:
   - Flame-sterilize syringe needle until glowing red
   - Allow to cool for 10-15 seconds (wave gently, do not blow)
   - Wipe needle with isopropyl-soaked tissue

3. Inoculate jars:
   - For modified lids with polyfill: remove foil, push needle through polyfill, inject 1-3 mL per jar
   - For injection port lids: push needle through silicone port, inject 1-3 mL per jar
   - Distribute inoculant to multiple points against the jar wall if possible
   - Use no more than 3-5 mL per quart jar (excessive liquid promotes contamination)

4. Post-inoculation:
   - Remove foil from lids (gas exchange begins now)
   - Re-wipe needle with isopropyl alcohol between jars
   - Re-flame sterilize needle every 2-3 jars

**Quality Indicators:**

- Sterile technique maintained throughout
- Even distribution of inoculant across jar
- No coughing, sneezing, or drafts during inoculation

**Control Points:**

- Flame sterilization of needle between jars is critical
- Avoid over-inoculation — too much liquid promotes wet rot
- Work quickly but methodically in SAB

**Troubleshooting:**

| Problem | Cause | Solution |
| ------- | ----- | -------- |
| Contamination in many jars | Syringe contaminated or poor SAB technique | Replace syringe; improve SAB technique |
| No growth after 2 weeks | Dead spores or non-viable LC | Test syringe on agar; replace inoculant |

### Step 5: Colonization

**Trigger/Timing:** After inoculation — jars stored in warm, dark area

#### Procedure:

1. Place inoculated jars in colonization environment:
   - Store in a dark or low-light area
   - Maintain temperature in optimal range
   - Keep jars away from HVAC vents and drafts

2. Monitor daily (observe only, do not open):
   - Look for white, ropy mycelial growth spreading from inoculation points
   - Check for signs of contamination (green, black, pink, or yellow growth)
   - Check for wet rot (black, slimy grain with sour smell)

3. Shake jars when partially colonized (optional but recommended):
   - When jars are 20-30% colonized, shake vigorously to distribute mycelium
   - This breaks up colonized grain and distributes it throughout the jar
   - Greatly accelerates overall colonization time
   - Only shake if no contamination is suspected

4. Wait for full colonization:
   - Jars are fully colonized when white mycelium covers all visible grain
   - Allow additional 1 week after visible full colonization ("consolidation period")
   - Fully consolidated jars are more vigorous and resistant to contamination when opened

**Growth Parameters:**

- Temperature: 24-28°C (75-82°F) optimal for most species
- Humidity: N/A during colonization (jars are sealed)
- Light: None required; indirect light acceptable
- CO₂: Elevated during colonization (jars sealed)
- Time: 2-4 weeks depending on species and temperature

**Quality Indicators:**

- White, dense, ropy or fluffy mycelium throughout jar
- No discoloration (green, black, pink = contamination)
- No foul odors (sour = wet rot; musty/cheesy = bacterial contamination)
- Grain no longer visible through the white mycelial mass when fully colonized

**Control Points:**

- Maintain temperature consistency — swings cause condensation which promotes contamination
- Never open jars during colonization unless absolutely necessary
- Discard any jar with obvious contamination immediately (bag and remove from grow space)

**Troubleshooting:**

| Problem | Cause | Solution |
| ------- | ----- | -------- |
| Green mold (Trichoderma) | Contamination during inoculation or sterilization failure | Improve sterile technique; extend sterilization |
| Wet rot (black, sour-smelling grain) | Over-hydrated grain or bacterial endospores | Dry grain more thoroughly; extend PC time |
| No growth after 2 weeks | Non-viable inoculant or temperature too low | Verify inoculant viability; raise temperature |
| Sectoring or off-color patches | Metabolite secretion or early contamination | Allow 1 week before deciding; discard if colored mold appears |

## Optimizations

### Efficiency Improvements

- **Injection port lids**: Pre-made lids with self-healing silicone ports eliminate the need for SAB during small inoculations and reduce contamination risk
- **Batch sterilization**: Run full pressure cooker loads (10-14 pint jars, or 6-8 quart jars per batch) to maximize efficiency
- **Pre-soaking grain**: Soak grain in water overnight (instead of boiling) for softer texture with less risk of splitting; then simmer 5-10 minutes

### Scaling Considerations

- WBS spawn scales well from pint jars to quart jars to half-gallon mason jars
- At scale, a large autoclave (or multiple pressure cooker runs) is required
- Grain spawn can be further expanded — colonized WBS jars can inoculate bulk grain (1 jar : 5 jars ratio)

### Resource Optimization

- Buy WBS in bulk (25-50 lb bags) from farm supply stores for significant cost savings
- Reuse mason jars indefinitely (replace lids each cycle)
- Spent grain spawn (after fruiting or used for grain-to-grain transfer) can be composted

## Community Notes

- WBS is one of the most popular grain substrates on Shroomery due to its low cost and wide availability
- The millet in WBS provides excellent nutritional profile for mycelium development
- Shaking colonizing jars significantly reduces colonization time (often by 1 week)
- Some cultivators add 1-2 tablespoons of gypsum per quart to prevent grain from fusing
- Jars with polyfill or Tyvek filter patches outperform simple nail-hole lids for gas exchange
- Colonized WBS spawn can be used to inoculate bulk substrates like CVG or straw at a 10-20% spawn rate
