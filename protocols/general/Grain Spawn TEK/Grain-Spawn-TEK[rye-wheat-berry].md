## Safety Information

[Follow general safety practices](/general-information/saftey/general-safety.md)

### Known Risks

- Bacterial contamination risk if grain is improperly hydrated or sterilized
- Mold contamination (Trichoderma, Penicillium) if sterilization or inoculation technique fails
- Burns from pressure cooker steam and boiling water
- Respiratory exposure to spores during fruiting

### Containment Requirements

- Pressure cooker capable of reaching 15 PSI is required (no alternatives for grain sterilization)
- Still Air Box (SAB) or Laminar Flow Hood required for inoculation
- Clean, dedicated workspace away from high-traffic areas

### Safety Equipment

- Nitrile gloves
- N95 or equivalent respirator during grain handling
- Safety goggles and heat-resistant gloves for pressure cooker handling
- 70% isopropyl alcohol

### Emergency Procedures

- Contaminated jars: seal in plastic bag immediately and remove from grow space
- Pressure cooker burns: cool running water for 10+ minutes; seek medical attention for severe burns
- Inhalation exposure to contaminated spores: move to fresh air; seek medical attention if symptoms persist

### Storage Safety

- Colonized grain spawn stores in refrigerator at 2-4°C (35-40°F) for up to 6 weeks
- For longer storage, grain spawn can be stored frozen (−20°C / −4°F) for up to 6 months
- Label all jars with species, strain, and date of inoculation

### Disposal Requirements

- Contaminated grain: seal in plastic bag, dispose of in garbage (not compost without heat treatment)
- Spent grain spawn: fully spent and cleared grain can be composted or used as garden amendment

## Required Materials

### Essential Materials

- Whole rye berries OR whole wheat berries (available at natural food stores or farm supply)
- Wide-mouth quart or pint mason jars
- Pressure cooker capable of reaching 15 PSI
- Lids with gas exchange (polyfill filter patches or Tyvek discs) and optional injection ports
- 70% isopropyl alcohol
- Still Air Box (SAB)
- Alcohol lamp, torch, or lighter
- Spore syringe or liquid culture
- Tin foil
- Optional: Gypsum (calcium sulfate) — 1 tablespoon per quart

### Optional Materials

- Liquid culture instead of spore syringe (faster colonization, easier sterile transfers)
- Injection port lids (self-healing silicone, eliminates need for SAB during inoculation)
- Flow hood (superior to SAB for large-scale work)
- pH meter (for water pH verification)

### Recommended Suppliers

- Rye berries: Bob's Red Mill, Whole Foods, natural food stores, Azure Standard (as of 2026-MAR: ~$1.50-2.50/lb)
- Wheat berries: Same sources (as of 2026-MAR: ~$1.00-2.00/lb)
- Mason jars: Hardware stores, Walmart (as of 2026-MAR: ~$1.00-2.00/jar)

## Protocol

### Step 1: Grain Preparation

**Trigger/Timing:** Day before sterilization (grain must be pre-soaked or boiled)

#### Procedure:

**Method A: Simmer Method (Faster)**

1. Rinse grain in cold water 2-3 times, discarding floaters and debris
2. Add rinsed grain to pot with 3x volume of water
3. Bring to boil; reduce to simmer for 15-20 minutes
4. Grain should be plump but NOT split — rye berries are done when they just begin to show a small split at the germ end
5. Drain thoroughly through colander
6. Spread on clean towels or baking sheet to surface-dry for 30-60 minutes

**Method B: Cold Soak Method (Less Risk of Over-Cooking)**

1. Rinse grain in cold water 2-3 times
2. Submerge grain in cold water at a 3:1 water-to-grain ratio
3. Soak for 12-24 hours at room temperature
4. Drain and rinse
5. Optional: simmer for 5 minutes after soaking for additional hydration
6. Surface-dry for 30-60 minutes

**Checking Hydration:**

- Bite a kernel — properly hydrated grain should feel chewy but not mushy
- Squeeze a handful — no water should drip out
- Grain should look matte, not wet and shiny

**Quality Indicators:**

- Grain is plump with intact outer kernel
- No split kernels (a few are acceptable, but excess split kernels promote bacterial contamination)
- Surface appears dry/matte
- Grain separates freely (no clumping)

**Control Points:**

- Over-hydration is the most common cause of wet rot contamination
- Under-hydration slows colonization — maintain proper hydration
- Remove all floating grain and debris

**Troubleshooting:**

| Problem | Cause | Solution |
| ------- | ----- | -------- |
| Split/mushy grain | Over-cooked | Reduce simmer time; test at 10 minutes |
| Grain still dry/hard after cooking | Under-cooked | Extend simmer time or soak longer |
| Foul odor from grain | Spoiled grain or contamination | Start with fresh grain |

### Step 2: Jar Preparation and Loading

**Trigger/Timing:** After grain has surface-dried

#### Procedure:

1. Prepare lids:
   - Install polyfill or Tyvek filter patches for gas exchange
   - Optionally install self-healing injection port silicone
   - Ensure all components are clean

2. Optionally mix in gypsum:
   - Combine 1 tablespoon calcium sulfate (gypsum) per dry quart of grain
   - Gypsum prevents grain kernels from fusing together after sterilization
   - Mix thoroughly before filling jars

3. Fill jars:
   - Load grain to 50-60% of jar capacity
   - Leave ample headspace for mycelium and gas exchange
   - Tap jar lightly to settle, but do not pack
   - Wipe jar rim clean before applying lid

4. Cover with foil:
   - Wrap lid and top of jar with foil to protect filter patches during sterilization

**Quality Indicators:**

- Jars 50-60% full with surface-dry grain
- Clean rim seal
- Functional gas exchange medium in place

**Control Points:**

- Do not overfill — headspace is important for colonization
- Foil should be loose enough to allow steam to work around lid edges

### Step 3: Sterilization

**Trigger/Timing:** Immediately after jar preparation

#### Procedure:

1. Load pressure cooker:
   - Place rack or trivet in bottom of pressure cooker
   - Add water per manufacturer specifications (typically 1.5-2 quarts)
   - Arrange jars on rack above waterline

2. Sterilization cycle:
   - Seal pressure cooker
   - Heat on high; vent steam for 10 minutes with weight off
   - Apply weight/close vent and bring to 15 PSI
   - Maintain 15 PSI for **90-120 minutes**

3. Cool down:
   - Remove from heat; allow pressure to drop naturally
   - Do not force-cool (thermal shock can crack jars)
   - Allow to rest at room temperature for minimum 12 hours (24 hours preferred)

**Growth Parameters:**

- Temperature: 121°C (250°F) at 15 PSI
- Time: 90-120 minutes

**Quality Indicators:**

- Maintained 15 PSI for full duration
- No contamination visible after cooling
- Grain remains structured and not discolored

**Control Points:**

- Grain requires longer PC times than BRF — do not shorten below 90 minutes
- Full cooling is essential before inoculation

**Troubleshooting:**

| Problem | Cause | Solution |
| ------- | ----- | -------- |
| Wet rot (black, sour grain) | Over-hydrated grain or bacterial endospores | Ensure grain is surface-dry; extend PC time to 2.5 hours |
| Grain discoloration (brown/black) | Over-sterilized or water pooling on grain | Reduce PC time slightly; ensure jar orientation |

### Step 4: Inoculation

**Trigger/Timing:** After full cooling (12-24 hours post-sterilization)

#### Procedure:

1. Set up sterile workspace:
   - Wipe SAB with isopropyl alcohol and allow to evaporate
   - Wipe gloved hands with isopropyl alcohol
   - Minimize movement and air turbulence

2. Prepare inoculant:
   - Flame-sterilize syringe needle until glowing red
   - Allow to cool 10-15 seconds
   - Wipe with isopropyl-dampened tissue

3. Inoculate:
   - Insert needle through polyfill/filter patch or injection port
   - Inject 1-3 mL per pint, 2-4 mL per quart jar
   - Angle needle against jar wall to distribute inoculant
   - Re-sterilize needle every 2-3 jars

4. Post-inoculation:
   - Remove foil covers (gas exchange is now needed)
   - Store in colonization environment

**Quality Indicators:**

- Sterile technique maintained throughout
- Inoculant volume appropriate (not excessive)
- No contamination of any jars post-inoculation

**Control Points:**

- Flame-sterilize needle between jars to prevent cross-contamination
- Liquid culture is preferred over spore syringes for faster, more reliable colonization
- Work quickly and methodically — minimize time jars are "open" to air

### Step 5: Colonization

**Trigger/Timing:** Immediately after inoculation

#### Procedure:

1. Store inoculated jars:
   - Place in a warm, dark area with stable temperature
   - Avoid HVAC vents, direct sunlight, or areas with temperature swings

2. Monitor daily:
   - White, ropy mycelium should appear at inoculation points within 5-10 days (spore syringe) or 3-7 days (liquid culture)
   - Check for contamination: green, black, pink, or yellow growth
   - Check for wet rot: slimy, dark grain with sour smell

3. Shake jars at 20-30% colonization (optional but highly recommended):
   - Shake vigorously to break up and distribute colonized grain
   - This dramatically accelerates colonization (can cut 1-2 weeks off total time)
   - Only shake when confident no contamination is present

4. Wait for full colonization + consolidation:
   - Full colonization: all grain covered in white mycelium (2-4 weeks post-inoculation)
   - Consolidation: additional 5-7 days after visible full colonization
   - Consolidated grain spawn is denser, more vigorous, and more contamination-resistant

**Growth Parameters:**

- Temperature: 24-28°C (75-82°F) for most species; 18-24°C (65-75°F) for oysters
- Humidity: N/A (jars sealed)
- CO₂: Elevated (jars closed)
- Light: None required
- Time: 2-4 weeks to full colonization

**Quality Indicators:**

- Dense white mycelium throughout jar
- Strong mycelial smell (mushroomy, earthy)
- No contamination visible
- Grain fused together by mycelium into a cohesive mass when fully colonized

**Control Points:**

- Maintain temperature consistency
- Discard contaminated jars immediately — segregate from clean jars
- Never open during colonization unless necessary

**Troubleshooting:**

| Problem | Cause | Solution |
| ------- | ----- | -------- |
| Green mold (Trichoderma) | Contamination during inoculation | Improve SAB technique; check syringe |
| No growth after 2+ weeks | Non-viable inoculant or too cold | Test inoculant; raise temperature |
| Metabolites (yellow/brown liquid) | Normal metabolite secretion | Usually harmless; monitor closely |
| Partial contamination in one jar | Single jar contamination | Remove and discard; other jars likely fine |

### Step 6: Using Grain Spawn (Transfer to Bulk Substrate)

**Trigger/Timing:** After full colonization and consolidation

#### Procedure:

1. Prepare bulk substrate (e.g., CVG, straw, pasteurized hardwood):
   - Target field capacity moisture level
   - Pasteurize or sterilize as appropriate for substrate type

2. Break up grain spawn:
   - Shake/break colonized grain spawn into individual kernels inside bag or jar
   - Grain spawn should break apart easily when colonized

3. Mix spawn into substrate:
   - Combine spawn and substrate in a monotub or bag
   - Typical spawn rate: 10-20% of total weight (1 lb spawn per 5-10 lb substrate)
   - Mix thoroughly for even distribution

4. Colonize bulk substrate:
   - Cover and allow to colonize at same temperature as grain colonization
   - Bulk substrate should colonize in 1-2 weeks
   - Proceed to fruiting once fully colonized

**Quality Indicators:**

- Even spawn distribution throughout substrate
- White mycelium running through substrate within 1 week
- No contamination

**Control Points:**

- Higher spawn rates reduce contamination risk but cost more spawn
- Work quickly during grain-to-bulk transfers to minimize contamination exposure

## Optimizations

### Efficiency Improvements

- **Liquid culture inoculation**: Cuts colonization time significantly compared to spore syringes
- **Grain-to-grain (G2G) transfers**: One colonized jar can be used to inoculate 5-10 new jars, dramatically expanding spawn volume
- **Oven-dry grain**: Some cultivators use an oven (175°F / 80°C, 30 min) to finish drying grain before loading jars

### Scaling Considerations

- Scale from pint jars → quart jars → half-gallon jars → autoclave bags as operation grows
- Large-scale operations use autoclave bags with filter patches instead of mason jars
- G2G expansion allows exponential increase in spawn volume from a single culture

### Resource Optimization

- Buy grain in 25-50 lb bulk bags for significant cost savings
- Rye berries provide faster colonization than wheat berries for most species
- Spent grain post-harvest can be composted or used as garden fertilizer

## Community Notes

- Rye berries colonize faster than wheat berries for most species due to thinner hull
- Gypsum addition is strongly recommended by the community to prevent grain fusing
- Grain-to-grain transfer is a powerful technique to expand spawn without additional inoculant
- Many cultivators prefer grain spawn over PF TEK for higher yields and scalability
- Wheat berries are a good alternative if rye is unavailable — slightly slower but still excellent results
